# Values / political voice

## When to use this

Mission and values statements, donor appeals that argue rather than update, op-eds, about-us copy,
the "why this work matters" passages inside a grant proposal. Use it when the job is to make someone
believe something, not to inform them.

## What it sounds like

Morally direct and unembarrassed. It names systems and adversaries rather than describing "systemic
barriers." Anger is present but arrives as evidence, not as adjectives. It implicates the writer and
reader before it asks them for anything.

## Signature moves

1. **Say "humans," not "people," "individuals," "communities," or "beneficiaries."** And count them:
   "400 million unemployed or underemployed humans," "24,000 humans die from hunger." The noun
   choice is itself the argument, and it is used with total consistency. This is the single most
   portable fingerprint of the voice.
2. **Build the catalogue sentence, then snap.** Open with a flat declarative thesis, drop a colon,
   then stack four to six crises with "and… and… and, perhaps most worryingly" for 90–110 words
   before landing a short corrective. Accumulation, then snap. Breaking this into three tidy
   sentences loses the sense of a load being stacked on the reader.
3. **Stack anaphora across one-sentence paragraphs** with white space around them: "The next
   revolution will be one of…" four times over. It should read like speech transcribed, not prose
   composed.
4. **Pair a named vignette with a hard number, in the same paragraph.** Fixed formula: country, first
   name, age in parentheses, one physical detail, then the pivot to the global statistic. Never the
   story alone, never the number alone. Leave the vignette unresolved — nobody gets rescued.
5. **Make "we" accusatory as often as aspirational.** "That's why we walk past mentally ill and
   homeless people on the street corner." Institutional nonprofit "we" means the organization; here
   "we" means the species.
6. **Deliver anger as documentation.** Supply the mechanism and the figure — Citizens United, tax
   havens, an 8.2% effective rate — and only then name it in five words: "This is corruption, plain
   and simple!" Ration exclamation points to about one verdict per section.
7. **Preempt the "radical" charge instead of escalating past it.** "This is not a radical idea, but a
   reclaiming of the power of everyday people." Maximalist rhetoric, deliberately reasonable
   self-positioning.
8. **Turn to hope late, in the imperative.** After the bleakest passage, stack verbs the reader can
   act on today: "Organize, volunteer, start a local community space, start voting blocks, run for
   political office." No "imagine a world where."
9. **Bold the thesis sentence of each paragraph,** so the piece is legible at two speeds.

## Lines that exemplify it

> The next revolution will be one of **care**, of the **heart**, and centered on the simple belief that **all humans deserve a chance of opportunity, justice, love, and happiness.**

> This is corruption, plain and simple!

> This is not a radical idea, but a reclaiming of the power of everyday people.

## What to avoid

- "Beneficiaries," "target population," "the underserved" as a noun — see the terminology table in
  `../../voice-profile.md`, which always wins over an example.
- Passive constructions that hide who did something. This voice names the actor.
- Resolving a vignette to make the reader comfortable.
- Piling on emotionally weighted adjectives. Note the tension with `../../voice-profile.md`, which
  caps emotional language at roughly one weighted phrase per paragraph — **in grant proposals the
  profile wins.** Borrow this voice's structure and moral clarity there, not its full intensity.
